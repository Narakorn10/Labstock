## AI Diary (Required - 100+ words)

I initially made the ordering logic more elaborate than the operational policy justified. I treated a fifteen-day coverage window and a seven-day lead time as additive, producing a twenty-two-day forecast. That was technically coherent as a generic replenishment model, but it did not reflect the lab's actual routine of ordering every other week with a usual three-to-five-day delivery interval. The user correctly challenged the visible consequences: recommendations appeared early, reasons were demanded even without a quantity change, and the basis for recommendations was opaque. The worksheet exposed a second important mistake: applying two orders per month universally ignored rows that were intentionally ordered once monthly. I corrected this by using the worksheet values and explicit frequency markers, preserving audit history and avoiding virtual ISE rows. The key lesson is that lab workflow policy must drive the model; generic supply-chain assumptions must remain configurable safeguards, not silent defaults.

## Lessons Learned

- **Policy before formula**: Use the approved worksheet and real order cadence as the source of truth before applying generic coverage assumptions.
- **Separate concepts**: Ordering-cycle demand, delivery lead-time risk, safety stock, and expiry risk must be shown separately to users.
- **Production data changes**: Match item IDs exactly, gate the transaction on full matching, record history, and prove idempotence before declaring success.
